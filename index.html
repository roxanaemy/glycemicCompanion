Two numbers, two different questions. Most charts only answer the first one.

Glycemic Index (GI)

How fast the carbohydrate in a food raises blood glucose, measured against pure glucose (100). It says nothing about how much carbohydrate is actually on the plate.

Low55 or less
Medium56 – 69
High70 or more

Watermelon has a high GI of 76 — but a slice contains very little carbohydrate, so its real-world impact is small. That gap is exactly what glycemic load exists to close.

Glycemic Load (GL)

GI adjusted for the portion you actually eat: GL = GI × available carbs ÷ 100. This is the number that estimates the glycemic impact of the portion or meal.

Low10 or less
Medium11 – 19
High20 or more

Across a full day, a total GL under 80 is generally considered low and over 120 high. Meats, cheeses, oils and spices contain essentially no carbohydrate, so they carry a glycemic load of zero — they are listed here so you can build complete meals.

Four things that move the number

Published values are averages from tested subjects. Use them as a ranking tool, not as a lab result.

Ripeness

A green banana tests around 42; fully ripe, around 62.

Cooking

Pasta al dente sits far below pasta cooked soft; a baked potato beats a boiled one.

Cooling

Rice, potatoes and pasta cooled after cooking form resistant starch and test lower.

Company

Fat, protein, acid and fibre eaten alongside carbohydrate slow the whole response.

The recipe

For prepared dishes, brands differ enormously. Build the meal from ingredients when it must be right.

Sources. Glycemic index values are drawn from the International Tables of Glycemic Index and Glycemic Load Values (Atkinson, Foster-Powell & Brand-Miller) and peer-reviewed literature. Carbohydrate values reflect available (net) carbohydrate from USDA FoodData Central. Foods with negligible carbohydrate are shown as GI 0.

A note on mixed dishes. Entries in Prepared & Packaged are composite foods whose recipes vary widely between brands and kitchens. Where a tested value exists it is used; otherwise the figure is estimated from the dish's components. Treat these as an order-of-magnitude guide and prefer building the meal from its individual ingredients when precision matters.

This tool is an educational reference. It is not medical advice and does not replace individual guidance from a physician or registered dietitian, particularly for anyone using insulin or glucose-lowering medication.
